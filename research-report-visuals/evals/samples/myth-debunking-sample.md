# The "Mozart Effect": Does Classical Music Make You Smarter?

**Date:** February 2026
**Sources:** [Rauscher et al. 1993 Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/365611a0), [Chabris 1999 Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00185), [Pietschnig et al. 2010 Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2010.03.004)

## Executive Summary

The "Mozart Effect" - the claim that listening to Mozart's music boosts intelligence - became one of the most widely cited findings in psychology after a 1993 Nature paper. Thirty years of replication attempts reveal the original finding was real but trivially small, highly specific, and wildly misrepresented by media and commercial interests.

## What the Original Study Actually Found

In 1993, Rauscher, Shaw, and Ky published a one-page letter in Nature reporting that college students who listened to 10 minutes of Mozart's Sonata K.448 scored 8-9 points higher on a spatial reasoning task (paper folding) compared to silence. The effect lasted 10-15 minutes.

The authors never claimed Mozart makes you smarter. They reported a temporary spatial reasoning boost from auditory stimulation.

## What Pop Culture Claimed

- Listening to Mozart raises IQ permanently
- Playing Mozart to babies makes them geniuses
- Georgia governor Zell Miller allocated $105,000 to give every newborn a classical music CD (1998)
- Florida passed a law requiring state-funded childcare centers to play classical music daily
- "Baby Einstein" products generated $200M+ in revenue

## What Replications Found

- Chabris (1999): Meta-analysis of 16 studies found effect size of 1.5 IQ points (not 8-9), limited to spatial tasks
- Steele et al. (1999): Failed to replicate with larger sample
- Pietschnig et al. (2010): Meta-analysis of 40 studies, 3,000+ participants. Effect size: 0.37 SD on spatial tasks only. No transfer to general intelligence.
- The effect is now understood as "arousal and mood" - any enjoyable stimulation produces similar small, temporary boosts

## Timeline

- 1993: Rauscher et al. publish in Nature
- 1994-1997: Media amplifies finding far beyond what was claimed
- 1998: Georgia "Mozart for babies" legislation
- 1999: First failed replications and meta-analysis
- 2010: Definitive meta-analysis shows trivial effect size
- 2023: "Baby Einstein" brand still generates revenue despite refunded FTC complaint

## The Real Science

Music training (not passive listening) does correlate with modest cognitive benefits, likely through executive function, working memory training, and sustained attention development. The effect requires active engagement over months/years, not passive 10-minute exposure.
